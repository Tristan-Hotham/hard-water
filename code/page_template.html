Hard water

The world's water hardness, from {{stations.samples|int}} samples of rivers, lakes and groundwater, and the rocks behind it.

Dr Tristan Hotham · Independent researcher · blog.tristanhotham.com

Fill a kettle in Cambridge and in Aberdeen and, after a few months, only one of them will be furred with white scale. That scale is calcium and magnesium carbonate, and it got into the water from the ground. Rain is a very weak acid: it picks up carbon dioxide on the way down and through the soil, and that carbonic acid dissolves limestone, chalk and dolomite readily, while granite, gneiss and most sandstones barely give anything up. So the hardness of water is, more than anything, a map of the rocks it has passed through.

The familiar hardness maps of England and Wales show water supply zones, from soft in the west and north to very hard in the south and east. I wanted the world version. There is no open world map of tap water, so I used the next best thing: the UN's GEMS/Water archive of river, lake and groundwater measurements, {{overall.n|int}} stations with a hardness figure in {{stations.countries|int}} countries. Then I asked how well the rocks alone could guess the answer where nobody has measured.

    02 · WHAT THE STATIONS SAYA soft north, a hard middle

    Across all {{overall.n|int}} stations the median is {{overall.median|int}} mg/l. A quarter are very hard ({{overall.share_by_class.5|pct}} over 300 mg/l) and {{overall.share_by_class.0|pct}} are soft. Groundwater is the hardest (median {{overall.by_type.Groundwater.median|int}} mg/l at {{overall.by_type.Groundwater.n|int}} stations), because it sits in the rock for months or years; rivers come next ({{overall.by_type.River.median|int}} mg/l), and lakes and reservoirs, fed partly by rain straight onto their surface and often in hard, old rock, are the softest ({{overall.by_type.Lake.median|int}} and {{overall.by_type.Reservoir.median|int}} mg/l).

    03 · THE ROCKS BEHIND ITGranite gives soft water; limestone gives hard

    To link each station to its rocks I gave every unit on the world geological map shares of seven rock groups from its description (so "limestone and shale" is half carbonate, half mudstone), and measured the share of each group within {{model.world_radius_km|int}} km of the station. The clearest signal in the measurements is not limestone but its opposite: where crystalline rocks such as granite, gneiss and schist cover more than four fifths of the ground, the median station has just {{by_crystalline.3.median|int}} mg/l, against {{by_crystalline.0.median|int}} mg/l where they cover less than a fifth.

    Measured hardness against the rocks nearby
    Median station (dot) and middle half (bar), by the share of the land within {{model.world_radius_km|int}} km that is crystalline rock (left) or carbonate rock and evaporites (right)

    The model then asks what hardness each rock group gives on its own. It mixes the groups by their shares (as waters from different rocks mix), lets wetter places be softer and lets groundwater and lakes differ from rivers. Fitted to all the stations, it says a river draining only granite and gneiss under a metre of rain a year would carry about {{d.typ_crys|int}} mg/l; only carbonate rock about {{d.typ_carb|int}} mg/l; and loose young sediments such as loess and glacial clays about {{d.typ_unc|int}} mg/l. Each doubling of rainfall makes water about {{d.rain_drop_pct|int}}% softer, groundwater comes out about {{d.gw_factor|f1}} times as hard as river water nearby, and lake water about half as hard ({{d.lake_factor|f2}} times).

    Typical hardness of river water from each rock group alone
    Fitted values, mg/l, at 1,000 mm of rain a year. The Britain and Ireland model uses four broader groups and is fitted to Irish stations only.

    04 · HOW GOOD IS THE GUESS?Right class three times in ten, close two times in three

    A model can look good simply by remembering its own data, so I tested it the hard way: the world was cut into 5 by 5 degree squares, whole squares were hidden, and each hidden station was predicted only from the others (spatial cross-validation). On that test the rock model explains {{model.world_cv.rocks_rain_and_type.r2|pct}} of the variation in log hardness, puts {{model.world_cv.rocks_rain_and_type.class_exact|pct}} of stations in exactly the right class and {{model.world_cv.rocks_rain_and_type.class_within1|pct}} within one class, and is typically out by a factor of {{model.world_cv.rocks_rain_and_type.median_factor|f1}}. Guessing the global median for everyone gets only {{model.world_cv.baseline_global_median.class_within1|pct}} within one class. Hiding whole countries is harsher still ({{model.world_cv.rocks_rain_and_type_leave_country_out.class_within1|pct}} within one class).

    Measured against predicted, each station predicted with its own 5 degree square hidden
    Log scales, mg/l. The shaded band is within a factor of two; the grid shows the six classes.

    Within one class: predicted class is the measured class or next to it. Typical error: the median factor between measured and predicted.

    The map's own detail matters as much as the model. Where at least half of the land around a station comes from a detailed regional map unit that names its rock, the model gets {{model.world_cv_by_confidence.high.class_within1|pct}} of {{model.world_cv_by_confidence.high.n|int}} stations within one class (R² {{model.world_cv_by_confidence.high.r2|f2}}); where it rests mainly on the coarse compilations it gets {{model.world_cv_by_confidence.low.class_within1|pct}} of {{model.world_cv_by_confidence.low.n|int}} (R² {{model.world_cv_by_confidence.low.r2|f2}}, no better than a flat guess). That is why the map fades those areas.

    The misses are informative. The model over-predicts in Norway (measured water there is typically {{d.nor_resid|pct}} of what it expects), Canada and Denmark, and under-predicts in Spain, the Netherlands, Serbia and Cyprus (by factors of about {{d.esp_resid|f1}} to {{d.cyp_resid|f1}}). The reasons will differ from place to place, from thin soils over ice-scoured rock to gypsum and salty groundwater, and this simple model cannot tell them apart. The biggest weakness is the map itself: over about half of the mapped land the world geology I used says only "sedimentary", without saying whether it is limestone or sandstone ({{d.sedu_share|pct}} of mapped land outside Antarctica).

    05 · BRITAIN AND IRELANDA sharper look at home

    The open archive has {{stories.ireland.n|int}} stations in Ireland but none at all in Great Britain. For both islands I used the far more detailed BGS and GSI bedrock maps at about 1 km, merged into four groups (carbonate and evaporite; sandstone, mudstone and other sediments; volcanic; crystalline), and calibrated the model on the Irish stations, keeping the rainfall effect from the world model so it would not stretch too far across Britain's much wider range of rainfall. Under the same kind of test (hiding half-degree squares) it explains {{model.bi_cv.rocks_type_and_world_rain_effect.r2|pct}} of the variation in Ireland and gets {{model.bi_cv.rocks_type_and_world_rain_effect.class_within1|pct}} of stations within one class, far better than the world model does at the same Irish stations ({{model.bi_cv.world_model_at_irish_stations.class_within1|pct}}).

    Modelled share of land in each class
    River-water estimate from bedrock and rainfall, 1 km squares

    The rocks alone reproduce the broad pattern: south and east of a line from the Tees to the Exe the modelled median is {{bi_tees_exe.south_east.median|int}} mg/l and {{d.se_hard|pct}} of the land is hard or very hard; north and west of it the median is {{bi_tees_exe.north_west.median|int}} mg/l and {{d.nw_soft|pct}} is soft. The hardest counties are those on the chalk: {{bi_counties_hardest.0.name|raw}} ({{bi_counties_hardest.0.median|int}} mg/l), {{bi_counties_hardest.1.name|raw}} ({{bi_counties_hardest.1.median|int}}) and {{bi_counties_hardest.2.name|raw}} ({{bi_counties_hardest.2.median|int}}). The softest are in the Highlands and Islands, at about {{bi_counties_softest.0.median|int}} to {{bi_counties_softest.1.median|int}} mg/l.

    Where the rocks and the taps disagree. The familiar supply maps show London and much of the Midlands as hard or very hard, yet this estimate puts London at {{d.london_bi|int}} mg/l. London sits on clay, but its water comes from the chalk beneath and from the Thames and Lea, which gather hard water from chalk and limestone far upstream; my window of {{model.bi_radius_km|int}} km cannot see that far. Much of the Midlands sits on Mercia Mudstone, which carries gypsum that my rock groups count as plain mudstone. And supply zones often take water from far away. Water travels; rocks do not. There is no single open dataset of tap water hardness by supply zone for the UK: each water company publishes its own lookup.

    06 · FIVE PLACESStories in the water

    The chalk of the south-east

    {{bi_counties_hardest.1.median|int}} mg/lmodelled median, {{bi_counties_hardest.1.name|raw}}

    The chalk that makes the Chilterns, the Downs and the Yorkshire Wolds is almost pure calcium carbonate, laid down in a warm sea. There are no British stations in the archive, but the same chalk runs under the Channel into northern France, where {{stories.northern_france_chalk.n|int}} stations between Rouen, Amiens and Paris have a median of {{stories.northern_france_chalk.median|int}} mg/l and {{stories.northern_france_chalk.share_hard_or_vhard|pct}} are hard or very hard.

    The Yucatán

    {{stories.yucatan.median|int}} mg/lmedian of {{stories.yucatan.n|int}} stations, {{stories.yucatan.groundwater_n|int}} of them wells

    The peninsula is a slab of limestone with almost no rivers on its surface: water runs underground and surfaces in cenotes. {{stories.yucatan.share_vhard|pct}} of its stations are very hard. The world model misses this badly, at about {{stories.yucatan.model_map_median|int}} mg/l, because the map I used calls the rock simply "Eocene sedimentary". A good example of a model that is only as good as its map.

    South China's karst

    0stations in the open archive

    Guangxi and Guizhou hold some of the world's most famous limestone landscapes, the tower karst of Guilin among them. Here I have neither measurements nor a useful map: the world geology describes {{d.china_sedu|pct}} of the area only as sedimentary rock of a given age, such as "Palaeozoic sedimentary rocks", so the estimate (about {{stories.south_china.pred_median|int}} mg/l) is probably too soft. I leave it on the map as a reminder of what this method cannot see.

    Scandinavia and the Shield

    {{stories.scandinavia.median|int}} mg/lmedian of {{stories.scandinavia.n|int}} Nordic stations; {{stories.canada.shield_median|int}} mg/l on the Canadian Shield

    Norway, Sweden and Finland sit on some of the oldest crystalline rock in Europe, scraped bare by ice. {{stories.scandinavia.share_soft|pct}} of their stations are soft. The Canadian Shield is the same story: {{stories.canada.shield_n|int}} Canadian stations surrounded mostly by crystalline rock have a median of {{stories.canada.shield_median|int}} mg/l, against {{stories.canada.other_median|int}} for the rest of Canada's.

    Ireland: a hard middle, a soft rim

    {{stories.ireland.high_carb_median|int}} against {{stories.ireland.low_carb_median|int}}mg/l: Irish stations on mostly limestone, and on almost none

    Ireland is a saucer: a lowland middle floored with Carboniferous limestone and a rim of older, harder mountains. Its {{stories.ireland.high_carb_n|int}} stations where at least half of the nearby rock is limestone have a median of {{stories.ireland.high_carb_median|int}} mg/l; its {{stories.ireland.low_carb_n|int}} stations on almost no limestone, {{stories.ireland.low_carb_median|int}} mg/l.

    07 · UNITSParts per million, and three kinds of degree

    Hardness is usually given as the calcium carbonate that would carry the same calcium and magnesium: milligrams per litre, the same as parts per million. Europe also uses degrees. One German degree (°dH) is {{d.dh|f2}} mg/l; one English or Clark degree is {{d.eh|f2}} mg/l; one French degree (°f) is 10 mg/l. The map's unit buttons convert everything. Where a station measured calcium and magnesium but not hardness, I computed it as 2.497 times calcium plus 4.118 times magnesium, both in mg/l.

    08 · METHODS AND CAVEATSWhat I did, and what to doubt

    • Stations. The GEMS/Water Global Freshwater Quality Archive (version 3, open subset) holds {{stations.rows.hardness|int}} hardness, {{stations.rows.calcium|int}} calcium and {{stations.rows.magnesium|int}} magnesium records. Each sample's hardness is the measured total hardness, or, where that is missing, calcium and magnesium measured on the same day ({{stations.samples_from_camg|int}} of the {{stations.samples|int}} usable samples). Two countries (Bulgaria and Estonia) report total hardness in other units; their values were about {{d.bg_factor|int}} and {{d.ee_factor|int}} times smaller than calcium and magnesium imply, so for them I used calcium and magnesium only. Elsewhere total hardness and the calcium and magnesium figure agree within 20% for {{stations.crosscheck.share_within_20pct|pct}} of {{stations.crosscheck.pairs|int}} same-day pairs. I dropped {{stations.samples_dropped_implausible|int}} values below 0.5 or above 10,000 mg/l. Each station's figure is the median of all its samples ({{stations.years.0|raw}} to {{stations.years.1|raw}}; half the stations have {{overall.samples_median_per_station|int}} or more). The licence field is empty on every row of this open subset, which the archive releases under CC BY 4.0, so I kept all rows. A few station names had accented letters lost in the copy I worked from; they show as "?".
    • Coverage. The open archive covers {{stations.countries|int}} countries, mostly in Europe, North America and India, with none in Great Britain, China, Russia, Africa or Australia. A country's figures reflect where its stations are.
    • Rocks. World: Macrostrat's generalised geological map tiles at zoom 4 (about 5 km), {{world_geo.units|int}} map units, each given shares of seven rock groups from its lithology text by fixed word rules; summed into a 0.1 degree grid. Britain: BGS Geology 625k bedrock; island of Ireland: GSI and GSNI 1:500k bedrock; 1 km grid. Superficial deposits such as glacial till are not used.
    • Model. Hardness = (sum over groups of a typical hardness times the group's share) times a rainfall term times a water-type factor, fitted to the logarithm of hardness with each station weighted by one over the number of stations in its one degree square. Rock shares are averaged over a square window reaching {{model.world_radius_km|int}} km either side of each place, for the world and for Britain and Ireland alike, chosen by cross-validation among windows up to 50 km (world) and 10 km (Britain and Ireland) so the maps keep local detail; windows of 100 and 200 km scored about the same or a little better (R² up to {{d.r200|f2}}), as you would expect for big rivers, but blur the map. {{model.n_model_stations|int}} stations have mapped rock within their window and are used in the world model. Rainfall: CRU CL 2.0 annual means, 1961 to 1990, half-degree cells. The maps show the river estimate; land with under {{model.dry_mask_mm|int}} mm of rain a year is left blank ({{model.land_share_too_dry|pct}} of land outside Antarctica), because almost no stations are that dry.
    • Confidence. The world tiles stitch together six regional maps. Two of them, the Generalized Geology of the World and a map of Africa, are very coarse, with single units the size of countries and straight clip edges where they meet the detailed Arctic, Europe, North America and Australia maps; these edges show as straight lines on the modelled layer, for example along about 50 and 60 degrees north in Asia and Russia. The Europe map also uses a few plate-scale legend units (named only after tectonic plates such as "East European, Scythian, West Siberian, Turanian plates") east of the Volga. Each estimate is marked low confidence when less than half of the mapped land in its window comes from a detailed unit: one from a regional map, not named only after a plate or sea, and with a lithology that is not just "sedimentary". That covers {{model.world_pred_low_conf_share|pct}} of the modelled land, including all of China, Mongolia and Brazil and most of Kazakhstan.
    • Honest limits. Monitoring stations are not taps. A window of rock around a point is not its catchment: big rivers carry water from far upstream. The world map is coarse and often names only the age of the rock. The model knows nothing of soils, climate beyond rainfall, pollution or treatment. Treat the modelled layer as an educated guess with the error shown above, not as a measurement.
