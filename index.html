Hard water

The world's water hardness, from 611,858 samples of rivers, lakes and groundwater, and the rocks behind it.

Dr Tristan Hotham · Independent researcher · blog.tristanhotham.com

Fill a kettle in Cambridge and in Aberdeen and, after a few months, only one of them will be furred with white scale. That scale is calcium and magnesium carbonate, and it got into the water from the ground. Rain is a very weak acid: it picks up carbon dioxide on the way down and through the soil, and that carbonic acid dissolves limestone, chalk and dolomite readily, while granite, gneiss and most sandstones barely give anything up. So the hardness of water is, more than anything, a map of the rocks it has passed through.

The familiar hardness maps of England and Wales show water supply zones, from soft in the west and north to very hard in the south and east. I wanted the world version. There is no open world map of tap water, so I used the next best thing: the UN's GEMS/Water archive of river, lake and groundwater measurements, 16,682 stations with a hardness figure in 34 countries. Then I asked how well the rocks alone could guess the answer where nobody has measured.

Not a tap water map. These are measurements of rivers, lakes and groundwater, and the modelled layer is an estimate from geology and rainfall. Tap water depends on where a supplier draws its water, how it blends sources and whether it softens it. Your water company's own postcode lookup is the authority for your tap.

    02 · WHAT THE STATIONS SAYA soft north, a hard middle

    Across all 16,682 stations the median is 202 mg/l. A quarter are very hard (26% over 300 mg/l) and 12% are soft. Groundwater is the hardest (median 276 mg/l at 3,861 stations), because it sits in the rock for months or years; rivers come next (199 mg/l), and lakes and reservoirs, fed partly by rain straight onto their surface and often in hard, old rock, are the softest (139 and 111 mg/l).

    Share of stations in each class, by type of water
    Station medians, all years

    By country, the softest network is Norway's (median 8.2 mg/l over 133 stations) and the hardest Belgium's (330 mg/l over 83), among the 28 countries with at least 20 stations. A country's figure says as much about where its stations are as about the country: Mexico alone has 5,242 of them, 2,173 in wells, while India's are almost all rivers. Switch to the modelled view to rank 132 countries by the estimate instead, including the many with no stations in the open archive.

    03 · THE ROCKS BEHIND ITGranite gives soft water; limestone gives hard

    To link each station to its rocks I gave every unit on the world geological map shares of seven rock groups from its description (so "limestone and shale" is half carbonate, half mudstone), and measured the share of each group within 10 km of the station. The clearest signal in the measurements is not limestone but its opposite: where crystalline rocks such as granite, gneiss and schist cover more than four fifths of the ground, the median station has just 34 mg/l, against 220 mg/l where they cover less than a fifth.

    Measured hardness against the rocks nearby
    Median station (dot) and middle half (bar), by the share of the land within 10 km that is crystalline rock (left) or carbonate rock and evaporites (right)

    The model then asks what hardness each rock group gives on its own. It mixes the groups by their shares (as waters from different rocks mix), lets wetter places be softer and lets groundwater and lakes differ from rivers. Fitted to all the stations, it says a river draining only granite and gneiss under a metre of rain a year would carry about 22 mg/l; only carbonate rock about 211 mg/l; and loose young sediments such as loess and glacial clays about 257 mg/l. Each doubling of rainfall makes water about 21% softer, groundwater comes out about 2.0 times as hard as river water nearby, and lake water about half as hard (0.48 times).

    Typical hardness of river water from each rock group alone

    04 · HOW GOOD IS THE GUESS?Right class three times in ten, close two times in three

    A model can look good simply by remembering its own data, so I tested it the hard way: the world was cut into 5 by 5 degree squares, whole squares were hidden, and each hidden station was predicted only from the others (spatial cross-validation). On that test the rock model explains 24% of the variation in log hardness, puts 29% of stations in exactly the right class and 64% within one class, and is typically out by a factor of 1.8. Guessing the global median for everyone gets only 38% within one class. Hiding whole countries is harsher still (61% within one class).

    Measured against predicted, each station predicted with its own 5 degree square hidden
    Log scales, mg/l. The shaded band is within a factor of two; the grid shows the six classes.

    Within one class: predicted class is the measured class or next to it. Typical error: the median factor between measured and predicted.

    The map's own detail matters as much as the model. Where at least half of the land around a station comes from a detailed regional map unit that names its rock, the model gets 68% of 10,363 stations within one class (R² 0.36); where it rests mainly on the coarse compilations it gets 56% of 4,430 (R² -0.12, no better than a flat guess). That is why the map fades those areas.

    The misses are informative. The model over-predicts in Norway (measured water there is typically 24% of what it expects), Canada and Denmark, and under-predicts in Spain, the Netherlands, Serbia and Cyprus (by factors of about 2.0 to 4.6). The reasons will differ from place to place, from thin soils over ice-scoured rock to gypsum and salty groundwater, and this simple model cannot tell them apart. The biggest weakness is the map itself: over about half of the mapped land the world geology I used says only "sedimentary", without saying whether it is limestone or sandstone (50% of mapped land outside Antarctica).

    05 · BRITAIN AND IRELANDA sharper look at home

    The open archive has 388 stations in Ireland but none at all in Great Britain. For both islands I used the far more detailed BGS and GSI bedrock maps at about 1 km, merged into four groups (carbonate and evaporite; sandstone, mudstone and other sediments; volcanic; crystalline), and calibrated the model on the Irish stations, keeping the rainfall effect from the world model so it would not stretch too far across Britain's much wider range of rainfall. Under the same kind of test (hiding half-degree squares) it explains 60% of the variation in Ireland and gets 82% of stations within one class, far better than the world model does at the same Irish stations (71%).

    Modelled share of land in each class
    River-water estimate from bedrock and rainfall, 1 km squares

    The rocks alone reproduce the broad pattern: south and east of a line from the Tees to the Exe the modelled median is 92 mg/l and 21% of the land is hard or very hard; north and west of it the median is 35 mg/l and 71% is soft. The hardest counties are those on the chalk: East Riding of Yorkshire (330 mg/l), Hertfordshire (280) and Norfolk (251). The softest are in the Highlands and Islands, at about 20 to 23 mg/l.

    Where the rocks and the taps disagree. The familiar supply maps show London and much of the Midlands as hard or very hard, yet this estimate puts London at 40 mg/l. London sits on clay, but its water comes from the chalk beneath and from the Thames and Lea, which gather hard water from chalk and limestone far upstream; my window of 10 km cannot see that far. Much of the Midlands sits on Mercia Mudstone, which carries gypsum that my rock groups count as plain mudstone. And supply zones often take water from far away. Water travels; rocks do not. There is no single open dataset of tap water hardness by supply zone for the UK: each water company publishes its own lookup.

    06 · FIVE PLACESStories in the water

    The chalk of the south-east

    280 mg/lmodelled median, Hertfordshire

    The chalk that makes the Chilterns, the Downs and the Yorkshire Wolds is almost pure calcium carbonate, laid down in a warm sea. There are no British stations in the archive, but the same chalk runs under the Channel into northern France, where 141 stations between Rouen, Amiens and Paris have a median of 301 mg/l and 96% are hard or very hard.

    The Yucatán

    514 mg/lmedian of 187 stations, 131 of them wells

    The peninsula is a slab of limestone with almost no rivers on its surface: water runs underground and surfaces in cenotes. 92% of its stations are very hard. The world model misses this badly, at about 117 mg/l, because the map I used calls the rock simply "Eocene sedimentary". A good example of a model that is only as good as its map.

    South China's karst

    0stations in the open archive

    Guangxi and Guizhou hold some of the world's most famous limestone landscapes, the tower karst of Guilin among them. Here I have neither measurements nor a useful map: the world geology describes 81% of the area only as sedimentary rock of a given age, such as "Palaeozoic sedimentary rocks", so the estimate (about 108 mg/l) is probably too soft. I leave it on the map as a reminder of what this method cannot see.

    Scandinavia and the Shield

    14 mg/lmedian of 571 Nordic stations; 5 mg/l on the Canadian Shield

    Norway, Sweden and Finland sit on some of the oldest crystalline rock in Europe, scraped bare by ice. 90% of their stations are soft. The Canadian Shield is the same story: 54 Canadian stations surrounded mostly by crystalline rock have a median of 5 mg/l, against 42 for the rest of Canada's.

    Ireland: a hard middle, a soft rim

    192 against 17mg/l: Irish stations on mostly limestone, and on almost none

    Ireland is a saucer: a lowland middle floored with Carboniferous limestone and a rim of older, harder mountains. Its 117 stations where at least half of the nearby rock is limestone have a median of 192 mg/l; its 103 stations on almost no limestone, 17 mg/l.

    07 · UNITSParts per million, and three kinds of degree

    Hardness is usually given as the calcium carbonate that would carry the same calcium and magnesium: milligrams per litre, the same as parts per million. Europe also uses degrees. One German degree (°dH) is 17.85 mg/l; one English or Clark degree is 14.25 mg/l; one French degree (°f) is 10 mg/l. The map's unit buttons convert everything. Where a station measured calcium and magnesium but not hardness, I computed it as 2.497 times calcium plus 4.118 times magnesium, both in mg/l.

    08 · METHODS AND CAVEATSWhat I did, and what to doubt

    • Stations. The GEMS/Water Global Freshwater Quality Archive (version 3, open subset) holds 473,383 hardness, 489,609 calcium and 461,585 magnesium records. Each sample's hardness is the measured total hardness, or, where that is missing, calcium and magnesium measured on the same day (184,864 of the 611,858 usable samples). Two countries (Bulgaria and Estonia) report total hardness in other units; their values were about 50 and 2,491 times smaller than calcium and magnesium imply, so for them I used calcium and magnesium only. Elsewhere total hardness and the calcium and magnesium figure agree within 20% for 95% of 247,576 same-day pairs. I dropped 401 values below 0.5 or above 10,000 mg/l. Each station's figure is the median of all its samples (1906 to 2023; half the stations have 18 or more). The licence field is empty on every row of this open subset, which the archive releases under CC BY 4.0, so I kept all rows. A few station names had accented letters lost in the copy I worked from; they show as "?".
    • Coverage. The open archive covers 34 countries, mostly in Europe, North America and India, with none in Great Britain, China, Russia, Africa or Australia. A country's figures reflect where its stations are.
    • Rocks. World: Macrostrat's generalised geological map tiles at zoom 4 (about 5 km), 4,668 map units, each given shares of seven rock groups from its lithology text by fixed word rules; summed into a 0.1 degree grid. Britain: BGS Geology 625k bedrock; island of Ireland: GSI and GSNI 1:500k bedrock; 1 km grid. Superficial deposits such as glacial till are not used.
    • Model. Hardness = (sum over groups of a typical hardness times the group's share) times a rainfall term times a water-type factor, fitted to the logarithm of hardness with each station weighted by one over the number of stations in its one degree square. Rock shares are averaged over a square window reaching 10 km either side of each place, for the world and for Britain and Ireland alike, chosen by cross-validation among windows up to 50 km (world) and 10 km (Britain and Ireland) so the maps keep local detail; windows of 100 and 200 km scored about the same or a little better (R² up to 0.27), as you would expect for big rivers, but blur the map. 14,793 stations have mapped rock within their window and are used in the world model. Rainfall: CRU CL 2.0 annual means, 1961 to 1990, half-degree cells. The maps show the river estimate; land with under 200 mm of rain a year is left blank (18% of land outside Antarctica), because almost no stations are that dry.
    • Confidence. The world tiles stitch together six regional maps. Two of them, the Generalized Geology of the World and a map of Africa, are very coarse, with single units the size of countries and straight clip edges where they meet the detailed Arctic, Europe, North America and Australia maps; these edges show as straight lines on the modelled layer, for example along about 50 and 60 degrees north in Asia and Russia. The Europe map also uses a few plate-scale legend units (named only after tectonic plates such as "East European, Scythian, West Siberian, Turanian plates") east of the Volga. Each estimate is marked low confidence when less than half of the mapped land in its window comes from a detailed unit: one from a regional map, not named only after a plate or sea, and with a lithology that is not just "sedimentary". That covers 71% of the modelled land, including all of China, Mongolia and Brazil and most of Kazakhstan.
    • Honest limits. Monitoring stations are not taps. A window of rock around a point is not its catchment: big rivers carry water from far upstream. The world map is coarse and often names only the age of the rock. The model knows nothing of soils, climate beyond rainfall, pollution or treatment. Treat the modelled layer as an educated guess with the error shown above, not as a measurement.
